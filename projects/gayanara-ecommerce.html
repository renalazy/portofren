About this dataset

Gayanara is a synthetically generated dataset published by NgulikData. It is used here to demonstrate the analytical workflow — auditing, SQL modeling, segmentation, and BI delivery — not to produce real business conclusions. Two findings on this page (flat category revenue, flat retention) are artifacts of the data generator rather than business signal, and are labelled as such where they appear.

Problem & Scope

What a fashion retailer would actually ask

Problem statement

Where is revenue coming from, which customers are worth re-engaging, and are the levers the business already pulls — discounts and courier choice — doing what management thinks they do?

Scope

Five tables, 10,586 rows, orders from January 2022 to February 2025. All revenue work excludes cancelled and returned orders; shipping is excluded as a pass-through cost.

Objectives

  • Establish a single revenue definition that reconciles to source before reporting anything.
  • Quantify growth and say whether it is volume-led or price-led.
  • Segment the customer base into tiers marketing can export and act on.
  • Test whether promo codes lift basket size, and whether any courier underperforms.
  • Deliver a dashboard and a deck that state their own limits.
Data

Five tables, deliberately messy

The dataset ships with the kind of problems a real extract has: duplicate email addresses, product categories written in a mix of Indonesian and English with inconsistent casing, and missing review text. That is the point — the audit is the first phase, not an afterthought.

Definitions

One revenue definition, reconciled to source

The most common way an analytics deliverable loses credibility is three KPIs computed three different ways. These five definitions are fixed once and used everywhere — in the notebooks, the dashboard, and the deck.

MetricDefinitionValue
Gross revenue SUM(order_items.subtotal_idr), excluding cancelled and returned orders Rp 1,296,768,000
Net revenue Gross revenue less order-level discounts Rp 1,265,340,379
Completed orders Orders not cancelled and not returned 2,521
Average order value Net revenue ÷ completed orders Rp 501,920
Units sold SUM(order_items.quantity) 5,442

Two decisions worth stating

  • Net revenue reconciles to the rupiah against orders.total_amount_idr. If it didn't, the discount logic would be wrong somewhere.
  • Shipping is excluded as a pass-through cost, and category revenue is reported gross — order-level discounts cannot be attributed down to individual line items, so category figures sum to Rp 1,296.8M rather than the Rp 1,265.3M headline. The two reconcile exactly; the gap is the Rp 31.4M of discounts.
Phase 1 · Data Quality Audit

Four checks before any number is quoted

Notebook 01_Data_Quality_Audit.ipynb — every query below is shown in full, with its output, on the rendered notebook page. Each check ends in a decision, and each decision is written down — including the one where the answer was "leave it alone."

01Null auditMissing values are concentrated in three columns, and only one of them constrains anything.

customers.phone — 5.0% missing (40 rows). The only material gap: it rules those customers out of any SMS campaign. Email-led campaigns are unaffected.
products.material — 3.0% missing (9 rows). Low impact; needs a default label in product-mix breakdowns.
reviews.review_text — 8.0% missing (120 rows). Normal user behaviour — people leave a star rating without writing anything. Not a data defect.
-- One block per suspect column, UNION ALL'd into a single audit table. -- The customers block is shown; products.material and reviews.review_text -- follow the same shape. Full query in the notebook. SELECT 'customers' AS table_name, 'phone' AS column_name, COUNT(*) AS total_rows, SUM(CASE WHEN phone IS NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS null_count, ROUND( SUM(CASE WHEN phone IS NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) * 100.0 / COUNT(*), 2 ) AS null_percentage FROM customers UNION ALL -- ... products.material, reviews.review_text ;

02Duplicate customersThree email addresses map to two customer_ids each — and I deliberately did not merge them.

Scale: 6 of 800 customer records, 0.75% of the base.
Decision: customer_id is retained as the analytical grain, preserving the source system's own definition of a customer. Merging on email would silently invent a different entity than the one the business transacts with.
Follow-up: flagged for upstream data engineering as an account-creation issue. Documented here as an explicit decision rather than left to look like an oversight.
SELECT email, COUNT(customer_id) AS account_count FROM customers GROUP BY email HAVING COUNT(customer_id) > 1 ORDER BY account_count DESC;

03Category standardizationFourteen distinct category values collapse to six real ones.

The mess: mixed Indonesian and English terms (Aksesoris vs Accessories) plus inconsistent casing (Dress vs dress). Left alone, every category revenue figure would be split across duplicate buckets.
The fix: a CASE WHEN mapping to six clean categories — Jacket, Pants, Accessories, Dress, Shirt, T-Shirt — persisted as a new products.category_clean column so downstream queries never re-derive it.
-- 1. Add a column to hold the standardized category ALTER TABLE products ADD COLUMN category_clean VARCHAR; -- 2. Map every raw spelling — Indonesian and English, any casing — to one label UPDATE products SET category_clean = CASE WHEN LOWER(category) IN ('aksesoris', 'accessories') THEN 'Accessories' WHEN LOWER(category) IN ('celana', 'pants') THEN 'Pants' WHEN LOWER(category) IN ('jaket', 'jacket') THEN 'Jacket' WHEN LOWER(category) IN ('kemeja', 'shirt') THEN 'Shirt' WHEN LOWER(category) IN ('kaos', 't-shirt', 'tshirt') THEN 'T-Shirt' WHEN LOWER(category) IN ('dress') THEN 'Dress' ELSE 'Other' END; -- 3. Verify: 'Other' must come back empty, or the mapping missed a spelling SELECT category_clean, COUNT(*) AS count FROM products GROUP BY category_clean;

04Referential integrityZero orphaned rows — which is what makes the rest of the analysis safe.

0 of 4,986 order_items rows fail to match a products row.
Why it matters: this is the check that licenses INNER JOIN for all revenue work. Without it, every product-level join would silently drop sales and no total would tie out.
-- LEFT JOIN, not INNER: an INNER JOIN would hide the very rows being counted SELECT COUNT(*) AS total_items_sold, SUM(CASE WHEN p.product_id IS NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS orphaned_items, ROUND( SUM(CASE WHEN p.product_id IS NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) * 100.0 / COUNT(*), 2 ) AS orphaned_percentage FROM order_items oi LEFT JOIN products p ON oi.product_id = p.product_id;
Phase 2 · Exploratory Analysis

Seven questions, seven answers

Notebook 02_Exploratory_Data_Analysis_EDA.ipynb — the rendered notebook page has every query with its result table. Each task below states the business question, the approach — including where the obvious approach would have been wrong — and the finding.

01Revenue trendsRevenue roughly tripled over three years, and the growth is volume-led, not price-led.

Question: what is total net revenue, and how are order volume and AOV trending?
Approach: a CTE aggregates to order level first, then joins. discount_amount_idr lives on the order, so joining straight to order_items would multiply the discount once per line item and understate net revenue on every multi-item order.
Finding: monthly net revenue grew from Rp 21.8M (Jan 2022) to Rp 66.3M (Feb 2025), roughly 3×. Orders rose from ~32 to 100+ per month. AOV showed no directional trend at all, fluctuating between Rp 390K and Rp 610K throughout.
So what: the business is growing by acquiring more orders, not by getting more out of each one. Anything aimed at lifting basket size is untested territory — which sets up Task 5.
WITH order_gross AS ( -- Aggregate to order level FIRST, so the order-level -- discount is not multiplied once per line item SELECT o.order_id, STRFTIME(o.order_date, '%Y-%m') AS order_month, SUM(oi.quantity) AS units, SUM(oi.subtotal_idr) AS gross_revenue, MAX(COALESCE(o.discount_amount_idr, 0)) AS discount FROM orders o JOIN order_items oi ON o.order_id = oi.order_id WHERE o.order_status NOT IN ('cancelled', 'returned') GROUP BY o.order_id, order_month, o.discount_amount_idr ) SELECT order_month, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS total_orders, SUM(units) AS total_units_sold, SUM(gross_revenue) - SUM(discount) AS net_revenue, CAST((SUM(gross_revenue) - SUM(discount)) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS BIGINT) AS net_aov FROM order_gross GROUP BY order_month ORDER BY order_month;

02Category performanceJackets lead on both units and revenue despite having the lowest average unit price.

Question: which categories drive volume, and which drive revenue?
Finding: Jackets top units sold (1,026) and gross revenue (Rp 224.0M) at the lowest average unit price of the six (Rp 218K) — a purely volume-driven lead. Shirts carry the highest unit price (Rp 263K) but rank 5th in revenue.
Caveat, stated up front: all six categories fall within 14% of each other in revenue. Real apparel catalogs are far more concentrated than that. This flatness is a generator artifact, so the category ranking should not be read as a merchandising signal.
-- category_clean, not category: the raw column would split Jacket across -- 'Jaket' and 'jacket' and rank a fragment of it SELECT p.category_clean, COUNT(DISTINCT oi.order_id) AS total_orders, SUM(oi.quantity) AS total_units_sold, SUM(oi.subtotal_idr) AS gross_revenue, CAST(SUM(oi.subtotal_idr) / SUM(oi.quantity) AS BIGINT) AS avg_price_per_unit FROM order_items oi JOIN products p ON oi.product_id = p.product_id JOIN orders o ON oi.order_id = o.order_id WHERE o.order_status NOT IN ('cancelled', 'returned') GROUP BY p.category_clean ORDER BY gross_revenue DESC;

03RFM segmentationThe headline isn't the segment sizes — it's that the median customer hasn't bought in 145 days.

Approach: fixed business-rule thresholds on recency, frequency and monetary value, benchmarked against the observed distribution (median recency 145 days, median frequency 4 orders, median lifetime spend Rp 1.69M, p90 frequency 6, p90 spend Rp 3.66M). Rules, not clusters — chosen for interpretability, since marketing has to be able to explain the list they're exporting.
Segments (787 customers): Champions 26 · Regulars 269 · At Risk (VIPs) 135 · New/Promising 14 · Lost/Inactive 343.
Honest caveat: the "Lost" cutoff of 120 days sits below the median recency of 145 days, so by construction more than half the base lands there. The segment is large partly because of where I drew the line — a 180-day cutoff would tell a calmer story about the same customers.
So what: dormancy is the default state of this base, not an edge case. The actionable slice is the 135 At Risk (VIPs) — high spenders who have gone quiet, including the single highest-spending customer at 129 days without a purchase.
-- Recency is measured against the dataset's own max order_date, not today. -- Anchoring to CURRENT_DATE would make every customer look dormant purely -- because the extract is historical. WITH rfm_base AS ( SELECT c.email, CAST((SELECT MAX(order_date) FROM orders) AS DATE) - CAST(MAX(o.order_date) AS DATE) AS recency_days, COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS frequency, SUM(o.total_amount_idr) AS monetary_value FROM orders o JOIN customers c ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id GROUP BY o.customer_id, c.email ) SELECT email, recency_days, frequency, monetary_value, CASE WHEN recency_days <= 60 AND frequency >= 5 AND monetary_value >= 4000000 THEN 'Champions' WHEN recency_days > 60 AND (frequency >= 5 OR monetary_value >= 4000000) THEN 'At Risk (VIPs)' WHEN recency_days <= 30 AND frequency < 3 THEN 'New / Promising' WHEN recency_days >= 120 THEN 'Lost / Inactive' ELSE 'Regulars' END AS customer_segment FROM rfm_base ORDER BY monetary_value DESC;

04GeographyRevenue is spread evenly across cities — but encoding AOV as colour surfaces one outlier.

Top five by revenue: Semarang Rp 77M · Depok Rp 76M · Medan Rp 75M · Bekasi Rp 70M · Manado Rp 69M.
The design choice that earns its keep: the dashboard colours each city by AOV rather than revenue. That immediately exposes Bekasi — 4th in total revenue, but the lowest AOV of the top five (Rp 461K vs Rp 543K in Semarang). Same revenue rank, very different order economics.
So what: Bekasi's revenue is being carried by order count, not basket size — a different ad-budget argument than the cities above it.
-- AOV is computed per city here rather than averaged in the BI layer: -- averaging order values across cities would weight small cities equally SELECT shipping_province, shipping_city, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS total_orders, SUM(total_amount_idr) AS total_revenue, CAST(SUM(total_amount_idr) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS BIGINT) AS average_order_value FROM orders WHERE order_status NOT IN ('cancelled', 'returned') GROUP BY shipping_province, shipping_city ORDER BY total_revenue DESC LIMIT 15;

05Promo impactThe naive comparison said promos shrink baskets. The naive comparison was broken.

The trap: comparing total_amount_idr between promo and organic orders shows promo AOV at Rp 484K against Rp 514K organic — an apparent 6% penalty. But total_amount_idr is recorded net of discount. The discount is subtracted from promo orders and then read back as a smaller basket. The metric is measuring the discount, not behaviour.
The correction: add the discount back to put both groups on a like-for-like gross basis. Promo orders then average Rp 515,199 against Rp 513,830 organic — a 0.3% difference, which is effectively zero.
The real finding: Rp 31.4M was spent on discounts across 1,025 promo orders and produced no measurable basket lift. Not a penalty — a null result, which is the more useful answer and the more expensive one.
Recommendation: replace flat discounts with threshold-based promos — "Save Rp 50K on orders over Rp 600K" — which structurally require a larger basket to unlock, instead of rewarding purchases that were happening anyway.
-- total_amount_idr is stored NET of discount. Comparing it across the two -- groups measures the discount itself, not basket behaviour — so gross_aov -- adds the discount back and puts both groups on the same footing. SELECT CASE WHEN promo_code IS NULL THEN 'No Promo' ELSE 'Used Promo' END AS promo_status, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS total_orders, SUM(total_amount_idr) AS net_revenue, SUM(COALESCE(discount_amount_idr, 0)) AS total_discount_given, CAST(SUM(total_amount_idr + COALESCE(discount_amount_idr, 0)) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS BIGINT) AS gross_aov, -- like-for-like CAST(SUM(total_amount_idr) / COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS BIGINT) AS net_aov -- the misleading one FROM orders WHERE order_status NOT IN ('cancelled', 'returned') GROUP BY promo_status;

06Courier evaluationAnteraja has the worst cancellation rate and the best return rate — which points at a specific problem, not a bad partner.

Finding: Anteraja cancels 14.33% of orders (44 of 307), the highest of five couriers, against a fleet average near 10%. J&T 12.39% · JNE 9.94% · SiCepat 9.73% · Pos Indonesia 7.45%.
The nuance: Anteraja simultaneously has the lowest return rate of the five (2.61%). Damage in transit would raise returns, not cancellations. The pattern points to friction before dispatch — pickup delays or capacity rejections — rather than mishandling.
Recommendation, scoped to the evidence: run a dispatch-time audit on Anteraja orders and shift volume in one region as a controlled test. Explicitly not a contract decision — 307 orders is too thin a sample to renegotiate on, and no significance testing was performed.
-- No status filter on this one, deliberately: cancelled and returned orders -- are the measurement here, so excluding them would zero out the metric SELECT courier, COUNT(order_id) AS total_orders, SUM(CASE WHEN order_status = 'returned' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS returned_orders, SUM(CASE WHEN order_status = 'cancelled' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS cancelled_orders, ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN order_status = 'returned' THEN 1.0 ELSE 0 END) / COUNT(order_id) * 100, 2) AS return_rate_pct, ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN order_status = 'cancelled' THEN 1.0 ELSE 0 END) / COUNT(order_id) * 100, 2) AS cancel_rate_pct FROM orders GROUP BY courier ORDER BY return_rate_pct DESC;

07Cohort retentionThe flat retention curve is the most interesting result on this page — and it is a finding about the data, not the business.

Finding: pooled across cohorts, repeat purchase rate holds between 8.1% and 12.6% from Month 1 through Month 12, with no downward slope. M1 9.15% · M6 10.32% · M12 10.40%.
Why that's wrong: real e-commerce retention curves drop steeply in Month 1 and then flatten out. A curve that is flat from the start means a customer's tenure carries no information about whether they'll buy next month — which is the signature of independent random draws, not of customer lifecycle behaviour.
The honest read: this is diagnostic of the synthetic generator. It would have been easy — and completely wrong — to present it as a churn crisis. Instead it sets a hard boundary: this dataset cannot support churn analysis at all, so churn drivers are out of scope rather than badly estimated.
WITH first_purchases AS ( -- Step 1: first month a customer made a COMPLETED purchase. The status -- filter matters twice over: without it a cancelled order can define the -- cohort month, and cancelled orders then count as "retention". SELECT customer_id, DATE_TRUNC('month', MIN(order_date)) AS cohort_month FROM orders WHERE order_status NOT IN ('cancelled', 'returned') GROUP BY customer_id ), cohort_data AS ( -- Step 2: every later completed purchase month SELECT f.cohort_month, DATE_TRUNC('month', o.order_date) AS activity_month, o.customer_id FROM orders o JOIN first_purchases f ON o.customer_id = f.customer_id WHERE o.order_status NOT IN ('cancelled', 'returned') ), cohort_sizes AS ( -- Step 3: original size of each cohort SELECT cohort_month, COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS initial_customers FROM first_purchases GROUP BY cohort_month ), retention_counts AS ( -- Step 4: months-since-first-order, and who was active SELECT c.cohort_month, DATE_DIFF('month', c.cohort_month, c.activity_month) AS month_index, COUNT(DISTINCT c.customer_id) AS active_customers FROM cohort_data c GROUP BY c.cohort_month, month_index ) -- Pooled across cohorts and weighted by cohort size, so a single small -- cohort with an odd month can't swing the curve SELECT r.month_index, SUM(r.active_customers) AS total_active, SUM(s.initial_customers) AS total_cohort_base, ROUND(SUM(r.active_customers) * 100.0 / SUM(s.initial_customers), 2) AS avg_retention_pct FROM retention_counts r JOIN cohort_sizes s ON r.cohort_month = s.cohort_month WHERE r.month_index <= 12 GROUP BY r.month_index ORDER BY r.month_index;

Recommendations

What I'd actually put in front of a stakeholder

Step 01 Restructure promos Rp 31.4M of discounts produced no measurable basket lift. Replace flat discounts with threshold-based offers ("Save Rp 50K on orders over Rp 600K") that structurally require a larger basket to unlock — and run the next one as a holdout test so incrementality is measurable rather than inferred.
Step 02 Win back At Risk VIPs 135 high-spending customers have gone dormant, including the single largest spender at 129 days. This is an exportable list, not an abstraction — target it directly before spending more on acquisition.
Step 03 Audit Anteraja dispatch Highest cancellation rate paired with the lowest return rate points to pre-dispatch friction. Audit dispatch timing and shift volume in one region as a controlled test — not a contract decision on 307 orders.
Step 04 Protect volume-led growth Growth is coming entirely from order count while AOV stays flat. That makes acquisition and repeat-purchase frequency the levers that matter, and it makes the flat AOV a deliberate target to attack rather than an accident to ignore.
Step 05 Fix duplicate accounts upstream Three duplicate emails is not a reporting problem today, but it is a signup-flow problem that compounds. Route it to data engineering rather than patching it in every downstream query.
Limitations

What this analysis can't tell you

This section exists because the value of an analysis is bounded by what its data can actually support, and saying so is part of the deliverable — not a disclaimer bolted onto the end.

  • No margin data. The promo analysis measures revenue impact, not true ROI. It is also observational rather than causal — establishing incrementality would require a holdout test.
  • Courier samples are too small. Anteraja carries 307 orders and Pos Indonesia 161. No significance testing was performed, so courier differences are directional signals for investigation, not conclusions.
  • No lifecycle dynamics in the data. The flat retention curve shows purchases behave as independent draws, so churn drivers are out of scope entirely rather than weakly estimated.
  • Three duplicate emails were identified and deliberately not merged. customer_id is retained as the grain; segment counts are directional at the account level.
  • RFM boundaries are fixed business rules, not statistical clusters. They were chosen for interpretability, and the "Lost" threshold in particular inflates that segment by construction.
  • Scope is January 2022 – February 2025, and February 2025 may be a partial month — read the final point on the revenue trend with that in mind.
  • The dataset is synthetic throughout. Flat category revenue and flat retention are generator artifacts. Nothing here is a claim about a real market.
