Bike Buyers Sales Analysis

Who actually buys a bike, and what separates them from the customers who don't? A ground-up Excel workflow — clean the raw export, pivot it three ways, chart each pivot, then wire the whole thing into a single filterable dashboard.

Overview

The dataset, at a glance

A raw customer export from a bike retailer: demographics, income, commute distance, and whether each customer purchased a bike — one row per customer.

1,026 raw customer rows in the original export
26 duplicate rows removed during cleaning
1,000 clean, deduplicated rows carried into analysis
48.1% of customers purchased a bike (481 of 1,000)
Problem & Scope

What the sales team actually needs to know

Problem statement

Which customer traits — income, commute distance, age — actually separate bike buyers from non-buyers, so marketing can target the right segment instead of guessing?

Scope

A single retailer export of 1,026 customer records (marital status, gender, income, education, occupation, home ownership, cars, commute distance, region, age, and purchase outcome), cleaned down to 1,000 usable rows.

Objectives

  • Standardize inconsistent raw fields before any analysis touches them.
  • Pivot the clean data by gender, commute distance, and age bracket to see where purchase rates differ.
  • Package the findings into a dashboard a non-technical stakeholder can filter themselves.

Step 1

Data cleaning

Nothing gets pivoted until the raw export is trustworthy and readable. Three fixes, each with a reason behind it.

Remove duplicates

Excel's built-in duplicate check flagged 26 repeated customer rows out of 1,026. Left in, they'd double-count those customers in every pivot table and quietly skew the average-income and purchase-rate numbers below.

Standardize categories

Marital Status arrived as raw M / S codes, and Gender as M / F. Spelled out as Married/Single and Male/Female, the pivot tables, charts, and dashboard slicers below are self-explanatory to anyone reading the file — no data dictionary required.

Format currency

Income came in as plain integers (e.g. 40000). Formatting the column as currency turns every pivot table and chart that touches income into something that reads like a business report on first glance, not a raw data dump.

Steps 2 & 3 · Pivot Tables + Charts

Key findings

Three pivot tables, each charted, each answering one question about what separates a buyer from a non-buyer.

01Buyers out-earn non-buyers on average, for both genders — the income gap holds regardless of who's buying.

Female — non-buyers average $53,440 vs. $55,774 for buyers.
Male — non-buyers average $56,208 vs. $60,124 for buyers.
Overall — buyers average $57,963 vs. $54,875 for non-buyers, a $3,088 gap.

02Purchase rate doesn't fall in a straight line with commute distance — it peaks at short-to-medium commutes and drops hard past 10 miles.

2–5 miles — 95 of 162 customers bought a bike, a 58.6% rate — the highest of any bracket.
0–1 miles — 200 of 366 customers, a 54.6% rate — the largest buyer segment by volume.
More than 10 miles — just 33 of 111 customers, a 29.7% rate — the lowest of any bracket.

03Middle-aged customers are both the largest segment in the dataset and the most likely to buy.

Middle Age — 701 of the 1,000 customers, buying at a 54.6% rate (383 of 701).
Adolescent — 110 customers, buying at a 35.5% rate (39 of 110).
Old — 189 customers, buying at just a 31.2% rate (59 of 189) — the lowest of any bracket.
Step 4

Dashboard & slicers

The three charts above sit on one dashboard sheet, connected to four slicers — the piece that turns a static report into something a sales or marketing lead can explore without touching a formula.

Why slicers, not filters

A slicer is a visible, clickable button panel instead of a hidden dropdown — connected to all three pivot charts at once, so one click re-slices income, commute, and age bracket together.

What's wired in

  • Marital Status
  • Region
  • Education
  • Occupation
Insights

Insight summary

  • Income correlates with purchase for both genders — buyers average roughly $3,000 more per year than non-buyers.
  • Commute distance is non-linear — the sweet spot for bike purchases is a 0–5 mile commute, not the shortest or longest bracket.
  • Middle-aged customers are both the biggest segment and the highest-converting one, while older customers convert the least.
  • The dashboard's slicers (marital status, region, education, occupation) let these three findings be re-cut on demand, without opening a new pivot table.
Recommendations

Where to focus the next marketing push

Step 01 Commute distance Prioritize customers with a 0–5 mile commute — this range converts at 54.6%–58.6%, versus 29.7% for commutes over 10 miles.
Step 02 Age bracket Lead with the Middle Age bracket — it's 701 of the 1,000 customers and converts at 54.6%, the highest of any age group.
Step 03 Income tier Buyers skew to higher income across the board — worth testing pricing or bundling that targets the upper-income half of the customer base.
Step 04 Keep the slicers live Use the marital status, region, education, and occupation slicers to re-test these findings as new sales data comes in, instead of rebuilding the pivots from scratch.
